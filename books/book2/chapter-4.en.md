# Chapter 4: The Weather Log

The harbor authority's records had come to the archive on permanent loan decades ago, and nobody had asked for them since. Nadia found the KSTL binders on the bottom shelf of the far stack, where the ceiling leaked and Gus kept a plastic tub to catch the drips. Three grey lever-arch files, spines faded to the color of dishwater, each holding one year of the station's operating log.

She carried the 2014 binder to the listening desk under the fluorescent tube that flickered and never quite died, and opened it. The log was a form. Every hour the engineer on duty initialed a line and noted transmitter power, modulation, weather. Most of the year was Gus's handwriting, small and upright, and seeing it there was like hearing a voice through a wall.

The third of March was a Monday. The page for that day held a single line, OFF AIR, STORM DAMAGE TO MAST, with no initials and no power reading. Someone had ruled a diagonal stroke through the remaining hours and written, in a different hand, resume when repaired, and then the next day began normally, as though nothing had happened.

Nadia sat still and listened to the rain finding the window. She had heard the tape. She had heard the carrier tone, the hiss, the storm behind the voice, the distress call from a ship that was supposed to be at the bottom of the sea. A station whose mast had come down in a storm could not put a man's whisper onto a quarter-inch reel.

Two facts could not both be true. One of them was a lie written in ink, and the ink belonged to this building.

She turned back a page, then forward, and checked the whole week. The handwriting after 3 March was not Gus's. It was rounder, more careful, the sort of hand taught to make its letters touch the line. She had seen it before, on the reel label, on the box of clippings she had carried down from her grandmother's attic, on a birthday card from 2013.

She closed the binder and held both palms flat on the cover until her breathing evened out.

The photocopier stood in the alcove by the stairs and warmed up with a sound like a kettle starting to complain. She copied the week, seven pages, then the two pages before and the two after, because she did not trust herself to be sensible. The green eye slid back and forth under the lid. Each sheet came out warm and faintly perfumed with toner, and she stacked them face down so she would not have to look at the line again.

Gus was in his office with the door open, which meant he was expecting someone. A tin of licorice drops sat by his elbow, lid off. He was doing the crossword in pencil and not doing it well.

She set the binder on his desk, open to the third of March. "The station wasn't off the air," she said. He looked at the page for a long moment, then took a licorice drop and put it in his mouth and took his time about it. "That's the record," he said. "The record is wrong." "Records get wrong. Storm like that, half the coast was dark. A man writes off a day, later he can't remember which day it was."

"Gus. I heard the tape." Something went out of his face, not fear exactly but a kind of tiredness, the way a man looks when a bill he has been ignoring finally arrives. He closed the binder. His hands were not steady, and he put them in his lap where she could not see them. "Where's the tape now?" "Safe."

He nodded slowly, as though she had confirmed something he had known and dreaded for a long time. Outside, a ferry horn sounded twice through the fog, low and patient, and the sound came down the stairwell and settled in the room like dust. "Be careful, Nadia," he said. "I'm not asking you anything and I'm not telling you anything. Just be careful who you ask."

She carried the binder back to the far stack. The tub under the leak held an inch of water; she emptied it into the floor drain and set it back. Then she stood a moment in the dark between the shelves, the photocopies warm under her arm, listening to the building settle.
