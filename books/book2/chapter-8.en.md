# Chapter 8: The Detective

The Marrow Bay police station sat on Harbor Street behind a stand of wind-bent spruce, its windows steamed and its gutters running. Nadia waited forty minutes in a room with plastic chairs and a framed photograph of a ferry that no longer ran, listening to the fluorescent tubes tick and the rain drum on the flat roof above her.

Detective Joaquin Serrano came down at last, a paper cup in one hand and a folder he did not seem to want under his arm. He was fifty-two, gray at the temples, wearing a cardigan under a jacket that had given up on him years ago. His eyes were the eyes of a man who slept in pieces. "Ms. Okonkwo," he said. "You called about a tape."

He did not sit down. He stood with his back to the window and the rain making a soft gray wall behind him, and Nadia understood that he had already decided how this would go.

"The case is closed," he said. "Twelve years closed."

"I know. I read the report when I was twenty-two." She set the laptop on the table and opened it. "I would like you to listen to eleven seconds." He looked at the laptop the way a man looks at a dog he is not sure of. Then he pulled out the chair and sat.

She played it: the hiss, the storm, the thinning, the voice. Put it down, Okonkwo. The speakers were cheap and the sound came out thin, but Serrano's face changed. Not surprise. Recognition, and beneath it something older, like a door opening on a room he had spent twelve years keeping shut.

"Where did you get this," he said quietly.

"Cormac Doyle's papers. His daughter donated them to the archive." Nadia kept her voice even. "The original is in the archive. Unlogged. It came in a biscuit tin."

He turned the paper cup a quarter turn, then back. "The Doyle file was thin," he said at last. "Three pages of narrative and a coroner's finding. I remember thinking it was thin even for a drowning." He breathed out through his nose. "When I pulled it again in 2019 there were pages missing from the evidence log. Signatures that went nowhere. Photographs referenced and not present. I filed a note and the note came back to me with nothing on it."

"And the Anselm?" Serrano looked at the rain. "The Anselm was never properly investigated. It was a foreign-flag freighter that was supposed to have foundered in deep water with everyone off her. Coast Guard did a surface search. No dive. No hull survey. Two of my requests for a marine casualty inspection were declined at a level I could not see." He said it flatly, the way a man recites a thing he has said to himself many times.

"Who declined them?" He did not answer that. He drank some of the coffee and made a face at it. "I was forty years old," he said. "I had a drowning with no body and a witness who was not a witness and a harbor full of men who all told the same story in the same words. I closed it in six weeks. I have thought about those six weeks every week since."

Nadia waited.

"You want me to say I will reopen it." He set the cup down. "I am not saying that. I am one detective and the file is a ruin and you are an archivist with a laptop and a grudge, and none of that adds up to a case."

"It adds up to a question." "It does." He nodded slowly. "So here is my answer. Rusk has friends. Not the son, though the son is worse than his father ever was. The friends are the ones who make a phone call and a form goes missing. You understand me?"

"I understand you." "Then understand this too." He stood, and for a moment he looked less tired and more sad. "I will not tell you to stop. I have no right to. But do not put that recording on the internet and do not play it for anyone in this building. If you find something, bring it to me and me alone, and I will decide what can be done."

He walked her to the corridor door. The rain had turned to a fine drizzle and the ferry horn sounded far off across the water. "Detective," Nadia said. "The pages that went missing. Were they about the boy?" He looked at her for a long moment and said nothing at all, which was its own answer, and then he went back up the stairs.
