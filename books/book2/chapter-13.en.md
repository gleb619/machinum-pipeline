# Chapter 13: The Care Home

Bellwether Hill sat above the city where the streets gave out and the pines began, and the rain met the roof of the care home in a steady, expensive hush. Nadia signed the visitor book with her real name, because false ones never held, and wrote "historical society" in the line for relationship, which was true enough.

The room smelled of antiseptic and boiled carrots and something older underneath, lavender maybe, or the ghost of it. Arthur Rusk sat by the window in a cardigan the color of oatmeal, a blanket squared over his knees, and he did not turn when she came in. Fluorescent light lay flat on the sill. Beyond the glass, a cedar shook in the wind.

"Mr. Rusk," she said. "My name is Nadia. I've come about the harbor." He looked at her then, and for a moment his face was perfectly polite and perfectly empty. "You're the nurse," he said. "You're the one who brings the pills." "I'm not the nurse." "The pills," he said, and turned back to the window. "They take the morning."

She sat in the chair across from him and set her bag on the floor and did not take out the recorder. For a while she simply sat. Rain ticked at the glass. A television murmured in the next room, a game show, applause like crumpled paper.

"My brother was Emeka Okonkwo," she said quietly. "He worked at the radio station. KSTL. Do you remember the station?" Arthur's hands moved on the blanket, folding and unfolding a corner of it. "Eleven forty," he said. Nadia went still. "What about eleven forty?" "Eleven forty," he said again. "It's in the log. It's written down in the log and nobody reads the log."

She reached down slowly and switched on the recorder in her bag, the little red light hidden under a fold of her coat. "Who wrote it down, Mr. Rusk?"

But the door had opened in his mind and closed again. He smiled at her with the sweetness of a man greeting a grandchild. "The water takes what it's given," he said. "That's all. That's the whole of it. The water takes what it's given and gives nothing back."

She asked him about the Anselm. She asked him about the Pelargic. He hummed, and the humming had a tune to it, an old song, and he watched the rain as though it were a program he had seen before.

Then he turned and looked at her with terrifying clarity. "You're the girl," he said. "The one that came after. He said there was a girl who listened." "Who said?"

But the clarity was already going out of him like a tide, and in the doorway a man in a dark coat was standing with a folded umbrella, and Arthur said, pleasantly, "Desmond. You're late."

Desmond Rusk came in and set the umbrella in the corner and shook the rain from his shoulders with the unhurried care of a man who has never once been wet. "Miss Okonkwo," he said. "The staff told me we had a visitor. I don't think my father's well enough for interviews." "He's been very kind," Nadia said. "He's been very generous," Desmond said, "with things that never happened." He smiled. "Walk with me."

In the corridor, under the flat light, by a window full of rain, he did not raise his voice at all. He said that the archive was a small department, that a complaint from Rusk Marine Holdings would be reviewed by the city, that her position was not permanent. He said the loan on the house on Salt Row was held by a bank that had been patient. He said her brother's memory was a delicate thing, and that the dead deserved better than to be made into a story on the news. He said all of it in the tone of a man discussing the weather.

"Are you threatening me?" Nadia asked. "I'm telling you what's already true," he said. "It's not the same thing. You're an archivist. You should appreciate the difference."

Behind them, from the room, Arthur's voice rose. "Eleven forty! Somebody write it down. Somebody write it in the book." Desmond's face did not change, but his hand tightened on the umbrella handle, and Nadia saw it, and he knew she saw it.

She walked out through the lobby and signed herself out and stood in the parking lot with rain collecting in her collar. Her hands shook on the wheel of the green Volvo all the way down Bellwether Hill. She turned on the radio out of habit. The tape deck was broken. The heater clicked and roared.

She was frightened, and she drove slowly, and she thought: he came because I was there, and he knew what his father had said, and he was afraid of an old man in a chair. That was the thing to hold on to. He was afraid of an old man in a chair, and that meant the old man still knew something worth being afraid of.
