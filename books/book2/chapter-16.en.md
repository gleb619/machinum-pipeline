# Chapter 16: Cinder Yards

Nadia parked the Volvo where the access road gave out, at a chain-link gate whose padlock had been cut and hung back on its hasp. Rain ticked on the hood. Beyond the fence the Cinder Yards opened out in the last gray light, forty acres of rusted rails and dead locomotives, weeds shouldering up through the ballast like something refusing to be done with the world.

She had come early, the way she came early to everything. The field notebook was in her coat pocket; the laptop and the digitized tape were in the canvas bag beside her. Three weeks ago she had been a woman who cataloged other people's sounds. Then the green glass float had appeared on her porch, and the ordered basement of her life had cracked open.

He came out from behind a row of boxcars, walking slowly, hands where she could see them. Gray had come into his beard, and he was thinner than the man she had watched through the shop window in Cape Verity. He stopped ten feet away and said her name, and twelve years came apart in the syllable.

"Em," she said. It was not a question. He crossed the distance and put his arms around her, and she smelled diesel and cold metal and the same soap he had used in 2014, and she struck him once on the chest with the flat of her hand, hard, and then she held on. "You let us bury you," she said into his shoulder. "I know," he said. His voice was wrecked.

They sat in the shell of a switching tower, out of the weather, on a bench somebody had dragged in years before. Sheet metal rattled overhead. A ferry horn sounded out in the fog and was swallowed. He told it in order, the way she had taught him to splice, one thing at a time, no gaps.

He began with the night of 3 March 2014, the overnight shift at KSTL, alone with the board and coffee going cold. At 11:40 the marine receiver caught a distress call: the Anselm, a freighter that was supposed to have gone down off the Narrows two weeks earlier, now taking water in deep water with a deckhand still aboard. Tobias Renn. A young man's voice saying a name, then nothing but carrier hiss.

"I called the harbormaster's office," Emeka said. "Arthur Rusk answered the line himself. He told me to stay off the air. Those were his words. Stay off the air, and log the night as storm damage. He said the ship was already gone and I'd only be broadcasting a dead man's voice."

"But you didn't." "No." He looked at his hands, at the grease under the nails. "I recorded it. Then I called Cormac, because Cormac knew the water and I didn't know who else to trust. He came down at one in the morning and listened twice. Then he asked me whether I wanted to be a witness for the next twenty years, or a man who was already dead."

The plan had been Cormac's. He took the Pelargic out himself and left her running in the shipping lane where she would be found. Emeka went over the side into a skiff moored at the cannery. By dawn the Pelargic was drifting empty and Elias Ward was already on the coast road north. Cormac told them the boy had gone out with him and fallen. He cried when he said it. Emeka thought he meant it.

"You could have sent one letter," Nadia said. "Any letter is a thread," he said. "They watched Adaeze's house for months. If I wrote, they'd have found me, and then her."

"Mama stopped saying your name. Twelve years. You gave her a funeral with no body in it and you didn't even stay." "I stood at the back. Behind everybody." He kept his eyes on the rails. "I heard the priest. I heard her not crying."

The anger came up through her like tide water, and under it something else, warm and unbearable, that she could not yet name. She wanted to hit him again. She wanted never to let go of his sleeve. Both were true at once, so she sat with them and let the rain fill the silence.

"Why now?" she asked at last. "Cormac's dead. You could have stayed Elias Ward forever." "Because he's dead, and because the reel is gone." He met her eyes. "Somebody took it from your office. They think it was the only copy. It wasn't." She waited. He shook his head. "Not tonight. There's one thing more. I'll show you tomorrow."

Nadia took the notebook out and wrote the date at the top of a clean page, because writing steadied her. Above them the fog pressed down on the switching tower and the rails ran away into it, dead straight, going somewhere she could not yet see.

"Then we finish it," she said. "Both of us. Whatever's left."

He nodded, and for the first time the face he had been wearing for twelve years seemed to come off him, and what was underneath was tired and thirty-eight and hers. They walked back through the wet ballast to the gate together, and the rain softened, and she did not let go of his sleeve.
