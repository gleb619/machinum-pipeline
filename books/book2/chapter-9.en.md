# Chapter 9: The Anselm

The rain stopped overnight and left the morning gray and low, the clouds sitting on the rooftops like a lid. The archive smelled of wet stone and the boiler, which clanked and sighed in its corner as if it were the only thing in the building with opinions. Nadia came in early, before Gus, and turned on the long row of fluorescents one bank at a time.

The harbor authority's records were on permanent loan and filled forty boxes in the north stacks, ledgers and manifests and inspection slips going back to the fifties. She had spent a week of lunch hours among them already. Now she pulled the shipping ledgers for 2013 and 2014 and laid them open on the worktable, and beside them she set the engineer's logbook from Kestrel Point, its pages soft as cloth.

The Anselm had been a coastal freighter of eleven hundred tons, built in 1974, registered to a company that existed mostly as a folder in a lawyer's office in another country. In the ledgers she followed her through three owners and two renamings, each transfer signed by the same notary, each company dissolved within a year of the sale.

In February 2014, according to the ledger, the Anselm went down in deep water twelve miles off Kestrel Point. The entry was four lines long. Crew recovered, no injuries, vessel a constructive total loss. The salvage claim was filed in the spring and paid within a month, and the wreck rights were sold on to a firm that appeared in no other ledger Nadia could find.

She worked back and forth between the pages with a pencil and her field notebook, and the shape of it began to come clear. The ship had been stripped before she went down. The manifests for her last two voyages listed cargo she could not account for and ballast she could not explain. A vessel of that age, in that condition, was worth more at the bottom than afloat. It was not a sinking. It was a disposal.

Then she found the casualty list, misfiled in a folder of crew agreements. Tobias Renn, deckhand, aged twenty-three, died aboard. The cause of death was given as accidental. There was no inquest, no witness statement, no name of the officer who had signed the entry. The handwriting was the same as the handwriting on the four-line sinking report.

She sat back and listened to the boiler for a while. The fluorescent light hummed at the edge of hearing, the note she had trained herself years ago to ignore.

In a box of insurance correspondence she found the settlement: a payment to Tobias Renn's mother, small enough to be invisible against the claim on the hull, large enough to be a year of her life. It carried no admission of liability. It required her signature on a page she was never given a copy of. The file number stamped in the corner ended in four digits, and Nadia looked at them for a long moment before she wrote them down. One one four zero.

Gus came down at nine with his coat still on and a bag of licorice drops in his pocket. He found her at the worktable with the ledgers open and the logbook beside them, and he stopped three steps into the room. "The Anselm," he said. It was not a question.

"The Anselm," Nadia said. "Do you know it?"

He put a licorice drop in his mouth and took a long time about it. "I know the name," he said, which was not the same thing, and went to his desk without another word.

She turned a fresh page in her notebook and drew the line again, the way she drew a signal chain: the ship, the stripping, the sinking, the claim. Off to one side she put the boy, and the mother, and the money that had moved quietly from one column to the other. Then, at the top, the date, 3 March 2014, and the distress call that should not have existed, and a voice telling her brother to put something down.

It was not proof. It was a diagram of a hole in the world, and she could see the shape of what had been taken out of it.
