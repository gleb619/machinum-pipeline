# Chapter 2: Hiss

Gus left at a quarter past five, and paused in the doorway with his coat over his arm. "Lock the front when you go," he said. "And the back. The alarm's been temperamental." "I know," Nadia said. "Set it twice if it argues." He held out the tin of licorice, then put it away when she shook her head. The outside door banged. The rain went on without him.

She waited until the building had settled into its evening quiet, until the last footsteps crossed the floor above and the library lights went off in rows. Then she unlocked the lower drawer of her desk and lifted out the biscuit tin.

The tape room was at the back, behind a door with a frosted window. The Revox A77 sat on its shelf under a plastic cover, heavy and gray. She uncovered it and ran her hand along the cold faceplate, then set the reel on its spindle.

Threading tape was a thing her hands knew better than her mind. Over the heads, under the tension arm, around the capstan, a neat loop on the take-up reel. She cleaned the heads with isopropyl and a cotton swab and watched the residue come away. It smelled like every radio station in the country. She plugged in the headphones, set the output low, and pressed play.

Hiss. Not silence, but the wide, breathing hiss of an open microphone in a room with rain coming down. She closed her eyes and turned the volume up until the room came through: a door moving somewhere, the buzz of a bad light, the storm outside like a hand held over the mouth of a jar.

Then a voice, thin and far away, cut through. "Mayday, mayday. This is the Anselm. We are taking water. Position..." The numbers came apart in the static. "We are taking water. There are men aboard. I say again, there are men..." The transmission stopped as if a switch had been thrown. In the gap the hiss came back, and under it Nadia heard rain drumming on a metal roof.

Then a second voice, close to the microphone, calm and unhurried. "This channel is closed. You will not repeat what you heard tonight. You will go home and forget the whole night." A pause. "Do you understand me?" Nadia's finger found the stop button, but she did not press it. She sat with the headphones warm on her ears and let the tape run.

And then Emeka spoke. It was a whisper. "Nadia." He said her name the way he always had, the middle syllable long. "Don't look for me. I'm all right. Look at the water. Do you hear me? Look at the water."

The reel ran out and began to slap against the end of its spool. She stopped it. In the sudden quiet the fluorescent lights seemed very loud. She was humming and could not remember starting. Her hands had gone cold inside the gloves, and she sat there while the whole building ticked around her, and then she rewound the tape and played it again, and it was the same. The Anselm. The order. Her name.

She was reaching for the volume when the light changed in the frosted window of the door. Gus stood there with his coat still on and his keys in his hand. He had come back for something and had not expected the back room to be lit. He pushed the door open and looked at the machine, then at her, and his face did a thing she had never seen it do.

"Where did you get that?" "It was in Cormac Doyle's boxes. Gus, listen—"

He crossed the room faster than a man of sixty-one should move and pressed stop. His hand stayed on the machine, and she saw it tremble. "That deck hasn't been powered in years," he said. His voice was dry. "Who told you to run it?" "Nobody told me. It's a weather log. I was assessing—"

"It's not a weather log." He said it too quickly, then closed his mouth and looked at the reel as if it might move on its own. He took a licorice drop from his pocket, unwrapped it, and did not put it in his mouth. "Gus. What did you hear?"

"Nothing." He switched the machine off at the wall. "Nothing. Go home, Nadia. Lock up. Set the alarm twice." He was at the door before she could answer, and then he stopped with his back to her. "Some doors in this building are better kept closed. Do you understand me?"

She did not answer. When the outside door banged, she stood alone in the back room with the reel warm from running. The accession ledger lay open on her desk to the day's page, Fiona Doyle's boxes entered in neat columns, with an empty line beneath them where the reel's description belonged. She did not write it. She put the reel in her bag, turned off the lights, and set the alarm twice. In the street the rain had thinned to a fine salt mist, and she walked to the green Volvo with her bag held against her side, deciding with each step that whatever was on that tape belonged to her before it belonged to the register.
