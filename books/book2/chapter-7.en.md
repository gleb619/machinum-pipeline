# Chapter 7: Cleaning the Reel

Rain came sideways off the bay all afternoon, striking the archive's high windows in long gray sheets. Nadia heard Fiona Doyle on the stairs before she saw her: boots, a coat shaken out, water scattering across the linoleum. Fiona came down carrying a canvas bag and the smell of the harbor, salt and diesel and cold weed.

She was twenty-nine and looked younger when she was angry, which was most of the time now. Her father had been dead three weeks and she had not yet found a use for her hands. Nadia cleared the second chair and set the reel on the worktable between them, in its cardboard box, the label in handwriting she still could not say aloud. KSTL 3/3/14, weather log.

"I need you to hear it," Nadia said. Fiona sat down without taking off her coat. "You said that on the phone. You also said you hadn't logged it." Nadia turned the reel over. The tape inside caught the fluorescent light, dark and faintly iridescent, like the wing of something preserved. "A man I work with saw me playing it and looked afraid. The station was supposed to be off the air that night." She stopped. "And because of my brother."

Fiona did not ask who her brother was. She had grown up six doors down on Salt Row, and the Doyle and Okonkwo houses had shared thirty years of the same weather. Grief in a small city is not private. "Because of your brother," she said, and it was not a question.

Nadia threaded the reel onto the Revox A77. The machine was older than she was, its heads worn soft, its capstan turning with the sound of a held breath. She had cleaned it twice that morning with a cotton swab and isopropyl, and the sharp clean smell still hung over the table. She set the speed, checked the tension arms, and let the tape run.

They listened to the storm first. Forty minutes of it: wind buffeting a microphone housing, rain like gravel on metal, the low boom of water against a hull. Fiona sat with her jaw set and her eyes on the turning reels and did not move.

Then Nadia ran the output through the borrowed interface into her laptop and captured the same stretch three times at three gain levels. "Playback is the easy part," she said. "This is the rest."

On the screen the storm was a solid gray band. She put a high-pass filter across it, notched out the mains hum, then ran a second pass to cut the hiss that lives above eight kilohertz like a thin continuous rain. The waveform thinned. Shapes began to show.

"There," Fiona said. A cluster of peaks beneath the wind, near the eleventh minute. Nadia looped it, slowed it, cleaned it again, and the voice came up out of the noise warped but unmistakable, a man speaking close to a microphone with the flat patience of someone used to being obeyed.

"Put it down, Okonkwo."

The room went still. The capstan whispered. Somewhere above them a gutter overflowed and drummed against the window ledge. Fiona's hand had stopped moving. "Play it again," she said, and Nadia played it again. "That is not a man in a storm," Fiona said slowly. "That is a man giving an order. He isn't asking." She added, quieter, "Okonkwo. Your family."

"My brother's name was Emeka Okonkwo. He was the night engineer at KSTL." Nadia's voice came out level, which surprised her. "He disappeared the next morning. The Pelargic came back empty."

Fiona stood and walked to the far end of the room, where the shelving ended and the wall was bare brick. She stayed there a long moment with her back turned. When she came back her eyes were wet and furious. "My father owned that boat," she said. "He told me he sold it in 2013. He told me he had never been to Kestrel Point in his life. There are boxes in my garage with his initials on them and a map of the north harbor folded inside, and I gave them to you because I thought they were junk."

"They weren't junk," Nadia said.

"No." Fiona pulled her chair closer. "He knew something. He knew for twelve years and he ate his dinner and never said one word to me. So. What do we do?"

Nadia looked at the reel, the laptop, the two of them reflected dimly in the dark window with the rain behind. Outside, a ferry horn sounded twice through the fog, low and long. "We keep going," she said. "But not alone. Not anymore." The tape ran on.
