# Chapter 1: The Donation

The rain came off the bay all morning, and Fiona Doyle arrived at the Fenwick Street loading door with her collar turned up and four cardboard boxes in the back of a borrowed station wagon.

Nadia met her under the concrete awning with a trolley and a sheet of plastic. The archive occupied the basement of the old Carnegie library, and its smell climbed the steps to meet them: wet stone, cold paper, the chemical breath of the dehumidifiers that ran day and night.

"You're the archivist," Fiona said. It was not a question. She was twenty-nine, weathered in a way that had nothing to do with the season, and she lifted the first box as though it weighed nothing. "Fiona Doyle. My father was Cormac Doyle."

Nadia knew the name. Everyone on the waterfront knew it. Cormac Doyle had owned boats for forty years, the Pelargic among them, and three weeks ago he had died of a heart attack in the lot behind the boatyard, forty feet from the water. "I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, and took the box from her.

Inside, the fluorescent tubes ticked and settled. Gus Pemberton sat at the front desk with the accession ledger open and a tin of licorice drops at his elbow. He had supervised the archive for nineteen years and had never once let a box past the door without a signature, a date, and a condition note.

"Sign here," he told Fiona, sliding the pen across. "Date there. No food or drink in the collection rooms. We'll give you a receipt for the lot." He looked at the boxes. "Cormac's, is it?"

"Everything from the office," Fiona said. "Harbor papers, tide tables, invoices. Some tapes. I didn't know what else to do with them."

Gus nodded slowly and did not look at the boxes again. Nadia noticed that. She noticed most things twice.

The work took the afternoon. She wore cotton gloves and used a soft pencil and acid-free folders, sorting by year, by hand, by the smell of the paper. Cormac Doyle had kept everything. Tide tables annotated in three colors. Receipts for fuel and rope and a new bilge pump. A notebook of weather, wind and swell and visibility, going back to the nineties.

The rain kept on. Above them the library was warm and full of people; down here there were only the lights and the dehumidifiers. Nadia had liked it for eleven years. The basement was the one place where nothing was asked of her but attention.

The biscuit tin was at the bottom of the third box, beneath a bundle of harbor authority correspondence tied with string. It was rusted along the seam and showed a lighthouse on the lid. Inside, wrapped in a plastic bag, lay a quarter-inch reel.

She turned it to the light. The label was written in pencil, in a hand she had not seen in twelve years: KSTL 3/3/14 — weather log. The slant of it. The way the four closed on itself. The pressure of the pencil. Her brother had written that label. Her brother had been dead for twelve years, officially, drowned on the night of the third of March, 2014, when the Pelargic was found empty off Kestrel Point and the town learned to say his name in the past tense.

"Everything all right?" Fiona said from the doorway.

Nadia heard herself hum, one low note, the way she did when the floor moved under her. She set the reel in the tin, set the tin on the shelf behind her, out of the light. "Fine," she said. "Just sorting. It'll take a few days to assess."

Fiona looked at her a moment longer than was polite. Then she zipped her jacket and went up the steps into the rain, and the door sighed shut behind her.

Nadia stood in the aisle with her gloves still on. The lights ticked. Somewhere in the stacks a dehumidifier clicked over and began to hum, and she did not write the reel into the register, not that day, not yet.
