# Chapter 17: The Confession

The hospice was low and brick, set back from the Bellwether Hill road behind a stand of wind-bent pines. Nadia signed the register and waited under a fluorescent panel that hummed at a pitch she could feel in her teeth. The nurse told her Vera Lindqvist had been asking for her since morning.

Room six smelled of antiseptic and rain. Vera lay propped against the pillows, small as a child under the blanket, an oxygen line under her nose. Her hands were folded on the sheet, spotted and still. On the windowsill, where she could see it, sat the green glass float, holding the gray daylight and giving a little of it back.

"You're the archivist," she said. "The one with the tape." Nadia said she was. "I saw your name in the paper. I thought, there's a girl who knows how to listen." She lifted one hand and let it fall. "Sit down. I haven't long, and I've been saving this up."

Nadia set the recorder on the bedside tray and pressed the two buttons, and the little red light came on. "You understand this is recording," she said. "You can stop it any time." Vera looked at the machine, then at her, and said, "Leave it running. My name is Vera Lindqvist. I was harbormaster of Marrow Bay in 2014. This is true, and I'll say it again if anyone asks."

She talked first about the harbor, the way old people do, about the winter the pilings went out and the ferry ran on one engine for a month. Then she came to it. "February 2014. The Anselm came in derelict and the owners wanted her in deep water for the salvage money. I signed the movement slip because Arthur Rusk asked me to, and we kept her at the buoy past the Narrows while the claim was prepared. On the night of the third of March we took her out ourselves and opened the sea valves. No survey, no company, nothing on the manifest."

"There was a boy aboard," Nadia said. "Tobias Renn." Vera's eyes went to the float. "Nobody was supposed to be aboard. He was asleep in the forepeak. We didn't know until it was done." Rain ran down the glass behind her. "I have thought about that boy every day since. That is not a figure of speech. Every day."

"There was money," Nadia said. "For his family." "Through a lawyer in the city. Quiet, no admission of anything. Arthur called it a kindness." Vera's mouth thinned. "It was a price. The mother took it because she had two other children and no husband. I signed the paper that made it legal, and I have never once written my name without thinking of it."

"And the third of March?" Vera was quiet long enough that Nadia checked the red light. "Arthur called me at home that night, from his house on the hill. He said the station was going to put something on the air that shouldn't go out, and it needed to stay off. I wrote storm damage in the log myself. I put the pen down and my hand was steady, and that frightened me more than anything."

"The Pelargic," Nadia said. "My brother." "I know whose you are. I knew the day I saw your name." Vera turned her head on the pillow. "When Doyle's boat came back empty, Arthur told me to keep it small. Missing man, presumed drowned, weather. No marine board, no search past the third day. I told the detective what to write, and he wrote it. Serrano. He was young. He has carried it badly, which is more than I can say for myself."

Nadia asked the only question left. "Why now?" "Because I'm dying, and I would like to stop being the woman who did those things. And because Arthur's son is tearing down the point and putting up glass towers, and after the ground is turned nobody will ask me anything again." She looked at the float. "Take that when I'm gone. It was my mother's."

"Can you say the last part again for the tape? About Arthur's order." Vera did, slower, in a voice going thin at the edges: the call, the order, the log, the inquiry buried, the money, the boy in the forepeak. The reels turned with a soft click, and under everything ran the hiss, that patient ocean sound Nadia had spent her whole working life listening past.

When it was done Vera closed her eyes. "You'll want to make copies," she said. "Do. And don't let anyone tell you a dying woman's memory is worth less than a piece of paper. I signed the paper. I remember better than the paper."

Nadia stopped the recorder, checked the counter, and pressed rewind. She played back ten seconds: her own voice, small and formal, and then the old woman's, clear as a bell struck underwater. She put the machine in her bag and zipped it, and took Vera's hand, which was light and dry as paper.

Outside, the rain had thinned to mist, and the ferry horn came up the hill from the water. Nadia stood in the parking lot with the recorder held in both hands against her coat, feeling its small weight, the confession inside it safe and turning, like something alive she had been trusted to carry.
