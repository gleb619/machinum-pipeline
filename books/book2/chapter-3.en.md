# Chapter 3: Salt Row

The house on Salt Row had been her grandmother's, narrow and gray and set into the hill with its face to the water. Nadia parked the Volvo at the curb and sat a moment with the engine off, listening to the rain on the roof. The tape deck had been broken for four years. The radio worked, but she rarely turned it on.

Inside, she put the kettle on and went to the back of the closet, where the box had sat through two moves and one divorce. It held what she had kept from 2014: newspaper clippings gone yellow at the edges, a folded program from the memorial service, a photocopy of the police case file that a clerk should not have given her.

She spread the clippings on the kitchen table under the light. FISHERMAN FEARED DROWNED, one said. SEARCH CALLED OFF FOR MISSING DECKHAND, said another, and she read that one twice before she remembered it was about a different man, on a different boat, in the same week.

The story was always the same story. A storm came in fast from the northwest. The Pelargic was found adrift off Kestrel Point on the morning of the fourth, engine cold, no one aboard. Forty-knot gusts. A life ring missing. No body recovered. The coroner's finding, six weeks later, was death by misadventure, and the file closed with a paragraph about the sea. The case file itself said the same things in flatter language: statements from the harbormaster, from Cormac Doyle, from a deckhand who had seen Emeka at the fuel dock that afternoon. Weather reports. A timeline that ended at eleven forty and began again at dawn.

She knew the file by heart. She had read it so many times that the photocopied pages had gone soft as cloth. What she had never had was a reason to believe it was wrong.

Emeka had taught her to splice tape when she was nine and he was thirteen, with a razor blade and a candle and a steady hand. He had worked nights at KSTL because nights were quiet and the pay was bad and he liked the sound of his own voice at three in the morning. He had been warm and careless and late for everything except the things that mattered. He had never in his life left without saying where he was going.

She put the clippings back in the box and drove across town to her mother's. Adaeze Okonkwo lived in a small white house on the flat side of the hill, with a porch and a lemon tree in a pot that had never once borne fruit. She was sixty-six and had taught fifth grade for thirty years, and she still stood as straight as a ruler. She made tea without asking and set it down with two biscuits on the saucer.

"You look tired," her mother said.

"I've been sleeping badly."

"You always sleep badly." Adaeze sat across from her, hands folded, and waited.

Nadia turned her cup a quarter turn. "I found something at work. In a donation. Something that might have belonged to Emeka." The name did its work. Her mother's face did not change, and that was how Nadia knew. Adaeze looked at the window, at the rain, at the lemon tree, and said, "We don't talk about that in this house."

"Mom." "We don't." Her voice was quiet and absolute. "I have buried that boy in my own way. I will not bury him again."

Nadia did not take the tape out of her bag. She had not planned to, but she understood now that it would only be taken from her, or worse, listened to. "I'm not trying to hurt you," she said. "Then leave the past alone." Adaeze stood and carried the cups to the sink. "It has teeth, Nadia. You think you're the only one who's been listening all these years? Leave it. Whatever you found, put it back."

Nadia drove home with the wipers going and the ferry horn sounding somewhere out in the fog. She hummed the whole way and did not notice until she stopped at her own door.

That night she drove back to Fenwick Street, let herself into the basement, and set the reel on the Revox. The borrowed interface was in her bag, a small gray box with two dials and a cable that ran to her laptop. She patched the deck into it, set the levels low, and pressed record. The tape ran. The hiss filled the screen in a green band. When it was done she saved the file twice, to the laptop and to a drive she kept in her coat, then packed everything away and drove home.

She sat at the kitchen table with the headphones on and played it back from the beginning. The rain, the room, the far thin voice of the Anselm. The order. Her name in the dark. When it ended she did not rewind. She sat in the narrow kitchen of her grandmother's house on Salt Row, with rain on the window and the refrigerator humming and the tape hiss still moving softly through the headphones, and she listened to it until she could not tell it apart from the sea.
