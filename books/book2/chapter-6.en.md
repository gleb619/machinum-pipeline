# Chapter 6: The Developer

He came on a Tuesday, in the afternoon, when the archive was empty except for Nadia and Gus and the hum of the dehumidifier. The rain had stopped and the light through the high basement windows was the color of weak tea. He knocked on the open door instead of walking in, which she noticed.

Desmond Rusk was forty-five and looked it in the way of men who pay attention. Grey at the temples, a good dark coat, shoes that had never been in weather. He carried no umbrella and was not wet, which meant he had come from a car at the curb. He introduced himself with a card held between two fingers and said he hoped he was not interrupting. His voice was low and unhurried, and he used her name often, the way a man does when he wants it remembered that he has taken the trouble to learn it.

Gus came out of his office with a licorice drop in his cheek, shook the offered hand, and called him Mr. Rusk, and Nadia understood that Gus already knew who he was.

They sat at the long table under the fluorescents. Rusk had brought a slim leather folder and set it down and did not open it. He talked about the North Pier development the way a man talks about a thing he has already built, the marina, the walkway, the shops, the tax revenue, the jobs. He said Rusk Marine Holdings wanted to make a gift to the archive. Fifty thousand dollars, unrestricted, for climate control in the map room and a proper digitization station.

"That's generous," Nadia said, and meant it, and waited.

There was, he said, one small piece of housekeeping. The harbor authority's waterfront records were held here on permanent loan, and with the redevelopment moving into its permitting phase, his people needed them closer, title, soundings, the old mooring surveys. He proposed a transfer to Rusk Marine Holdings for safekeeping, on loan, with a copy retained here. He had already spoken to the harbor authority. He said the words for safekeeping the way other men said please.

"Those records are public," Nadia said. "Anyone can read them here. There's a table and a lamp and a pencil cup."

Rusk smiled. It was a good smile, well made, and it did not reach the middle of his face. "Public is not the same as safe," he said. "Basements flood. I'm sure you know about the leak in the far stack."

Gus turned his licorice drop over in his mouth and said nothing. He was looking at the table.

"I understand your brother worked for the station," Rusk said to Nadia. "Emeka, wasn't it. Emeka Okonkwo. A quiet young man, from what I hear. Lost in the storm."

The room got very small. Nadia heard the dehumidifier, and the tick of rain in the downpipe, and under it her own pulse.

"I only mean," Rusk said, "that a family that has carried a thing like that for twelve years deserves to put it down." He turned his card on the table so it faced her, then thought better of it and left it lying where it was. "Some questions don't have answers. Some water is just deep. My father used to say that."

"Thank you for the offer," Nadia said. "The records stay where the terms put them. If the harbor authority wants them moved, they can write us a letter and I'll process it myself." Her voice was steady. Under the table her hand was closed so hard around the edge of her chair that her fingers ached.

Rusk stood and buttoned his coat and said it had been a pleasure, and that the offer would remain open through the winter, and that she should think about it. At the door he turned back. "Tell your mother the house on Salt Row is a good house," he said. Then he was gone, and the card lay on the table, and Gus let out a breath like a man surfacing.
