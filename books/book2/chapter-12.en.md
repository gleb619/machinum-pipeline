# Chapter 12: North Pier

She went to North Pier on the Thursday night, after the archive closed and after she had eaten nothing and could not think of a reason not to. The fog had come in thick off the water, and the sodium lamps made soft yellow coins of themselves in the air.

North Pier was the oldest of the harbor's fingers and the least loved. The warehouses had their loading doors chained, and the whole length of it smelled of creosote and cold iron and the green rot of low tide. A fence carried a rendering of what the pier would become, glass towers, and the rain had got under the plastic and made the towers look as though they were weeping.

She walked to the working end, where the pilings were black and slick and the water slapped at a ladder. She had a photograph in her coat pocket, printed from the newspaper archive: the Pelargic, the morning of 4 March 2014, tied up empty at a mooring on the north side.

It took her a while to match the photograph to the place. A warehouse with a broken gutter. A bollard with a chip out of its lip. And between two pilings, the mooring ring the Coast Guard report had described, still bolted to the timber, furred with rust.

She looked down at the black water. The Pelargic had come in on her own, the report said, drifting with the tide until someone caught her and tied her off. No one aboard. Her engine cold. A deckhand's oilskin folded on a locker as if put away.

"You're going to fall in." The voice came out of the fog to her left, and she turned too fast and caught the rail. An old man sat on an upturned crate with a fishing rod across his knees, in a canvas coat and a wool cap. He had been there the whole time.

"Sorry. I didn't see you." "Nobody does." He had a thermos and a bucket with nothing in it. "Tomas Bel. I keep watch down here. Somebody has to, now that it's all going to be glass towers."

She introduced herself, and he looked at her the way old men do when deciding whether you are worth the trouble. "Okonkwo. You're the archivist, the one asking about the boat." "I'm looking for where it was found." "It's where you're standing." He nodded at the ring. "I helped catch her that morning. She came in slow, on the flood, no lights, and I said to the boy, that boat's been left."

"Left," Nadia said. "Left." Tomas wound his line in a turn. "A boat that sinks, she has a story. She goes down with her gear out, her hatch open, her engine still warm. The Pelargic came in neat as a Sunday suit. Engine cold. Gear stowed. There wasn't a thing out of place but the man who was supposed to be in her." "The report said she was caught in the storm." "The report said a lot of things."

"Cormac Doyle owned her, and he was a liar and a chancer and I liked him fine, and he told me himself, down at the Anchor, that the boat did not sink. He said, 'Tomas, she was left.' He said it like a man who knows when to stop talking, and then he stopped talking."

The fog opened on the black water beyond, and there was nothing there at all. Nadia thought of Cormac Doyle in his last years, living alone with his boxes and his tapes, and of Fiona carrying them to the archive because she did not know what else to do.

"Did he say who left her?" Tomas shook his head. "He said the water takes what it's given. He said it wasn't the water took his boat. That's all. And then his heart stopped and the poor man went to his reward, whatever that was."

Somewhere out in the channel a ferry horn sounded, one long note and then a shorter one, the way they do in fog to say: here I am, stay where you are. The sound rolled along the pier and came back off the warehouses, doubled, and faded, and the water slapped at the pilings.

Nadia took out her notebook and wrote down what the old man had said, and he watched her write and did not laugh at her for it. "You're going to keep digging," he said. It was not quite a question. "I'm going to keep digging." "Then dig careful. There's people in this town who'd rather a boat stayed sunk, and a man with it." He turned back to his line, and the fog closed over him, and after a while it was as if he had not been there at all.

She walked back along the pier with the pages from the log under her coat and the horn sounding again, farther out now. The warning did not come all at once. It settled the way the cold did, into her collar and her cuffs and the seams of her shoes, and by the time she reached the fence she understood that the Pelargic had not been taken by any storm. It had been set down, like a thing set down carefully before a person walks away.
