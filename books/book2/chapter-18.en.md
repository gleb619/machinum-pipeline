# Chapter 18: The Second Tape

By four the rain had settled into a steady gray drizzle, and the bell over the door of Ward Marine Electronics rang once when Nadia came in. Emeka turned the sign to Closed and pulled the blind down over the glass. The shop smelled of solder and cold coffee and varnish.

He led her past benches of half-dismantled radios to the back room and moved the shelf of parts bins aside. In the wall behind it he had cut a panel, and in the cavity sat a steel cash box with a padlock. He worked the key and lifted out a cardboard sleeve.

The reel inside was quarter-inch tape on a metal hub. The label was in his handwriting, the same block capitals she had found on the canister in Cormac's biscuit tin: KSTL 3/3/14, MASTER. Beneath it, underlined twice, DO NOT ERASE. "The one in the archive was a dub," he said. "I ran two machines that night. Cormac kept the copy with his papers. I kept this."

"I told Cormac I burned it." He set the reel on the bench and did not look at her. "I lied to the one man who saved my life. I told myself it was in case somebody ever leaned on him." He turned the reel a quarter turn with one finger. "That isn't why. It was because I couldn't."

They threaded it on the shop's old deck, and he cleaned the heads with a swab that smelled of isopropyl, and the reel went around and the hiss came up out of the speakers. Nadia closed her eyes. It was the same hiss and not the same hiss; it was cleaner, the storm held further back.

The head of the tape had a station time tone, then his voice, young and careful, saying, "KSTL Marrow Bay, engineer's log, March third, twenty fourteen, eleven thirty-eight." A pause. A chair creaked. "Level check." The tone again. Nobody had cut any of it.

Then the storm. The receiver opened on a wash of static and a man's voice came through in pieces: the Anselm, taking water, position given twice, a name. Tobias Renn. The archive dub had given them nine seconds of him. Here he lasted almost two minutes, calm and then not calm, saying they were going down and asking somebody to call his mother, and then the carrier closed over him like water.

After that, a click and a line hiss. Emeka had held the telephone receiver against the microphone. Arthur Rusk's voice was dry, unhurried, certain of being obeyed. "Put it down, Okonkwo. This channel is closed. You will not repeat what you heard tonight. Log it as storm damage and stay off the air until I say otherwise." Then Emeka's own voice, younger, saying, "Yes, sir," and the click of the line going dead.

The last of it was the whisper, so close to the microphone that it was almost inside her ear. "Nadia. Don't look for me. Look at the water." Then nothing but the time tone, thirty seconds of it, and the reel running out. One pass. No splice, no stop, no gap.

Nadia sat back. Her hands were cold. "This is not a copy of a copy," she said. "It's continuous. Slate at the head, tone at the tail, the phone call in the middle. With Vera's confession, it's enough." Emeka nodded slowly. "Then we take it to your detective."

Her phone rang on the bench. Fiona, out of breath. "It's on the planning portal. Rusk Marine pulled the demolition forward. Kestrel Point comes down at first light. There's a crew staging at the Narrows now." Nadia put it on speaker so Emeka could hear. "That's the transmitter," she said. "That's the last of the station that isn't already rust."

She called Serrano. He was quiet a long time. "The permit is legal," he said at last. "I can't stop a legal demolition by wishing. I'd need a judge, and no judge is hearing anything tonight." He breathed out. "But if the transmitter is evidence, I can be there at first light as a police presence. I can make them stop once they've started."

"Once they've started is too late," Nadia said. "Then go tonight," Serrano said. "I'll meet you behind the library. Bring the tape and the woman's recording." The line went quiet. Emeka was already looking at the window, at the rain, at the dark coming down early over the water.

Fiona called back with the hour. The crew was due at the gate at six, an hour before first light. Dawn was 6:51. The coast road took two hours if the weather held, and the forecast said it would not. Nadia looked at the reel in its sleeve, then at the clock above the bench, whose hands she had to read twice before she believed them.

"We go tonight," Emeka said. "Now." Nadia put the reel in her bag against the laptop and zipped it, and they turned off the bench lamps one by one. Outside the rain had thickened again, and the ferry horn sounded from the harbor, and it was 7:12 in the evening. Dawn was eleven hours and thirty-nine minutes away.
