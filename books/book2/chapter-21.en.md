# Chapter 21: The Quiet Frequency

The archive was cold by December. Nadia wore fingerless gloves to splice, and the radiator clanked twice an hour and gave up, and the fluorescent tubes over the workbench hummed in a key she had never been able to name. She had always liked the winter down there, the whole building settling above her head. Vera's green float stood on the windowsill above the workbench, holding the gray light.

The register was a ledger the size of a suitcase door, bound in red buckram, with columns ruled in 1961 by a librarian whose handwriting had never been improved on. In October she had left a line blank in it, carried the tape home instead, and lied by omission to the one institution she believed in. Now she opened the ledger at the back and took up a pencil and filled the line in.

Accession number. Date received. Description: one quarter-inch open reel, seven-inch spool, polyester, approximately thirty-one minutes, recorded at KSTL-AM, Kestrel Point, 3 March 2014. Condition: fair; oxide soft at the head; splice at 12:40. Donor: Doyle estate. Notes: see also the Lindqvist recording, accessioned the same day. She wrote her initials in the corner, the way Gus did, and then, because the rules said so, she wrote the tape's title in the description field.

Then she did the work properly, which took a week. She cleaned the tape with a chamois and a little isopropyl, turn by turn, until the pads came away clean.

She cut out the failing splice and made a new one on the block with a razor and a candle, as her brother had taught her when she was eleven. She wound the reel onto a new spool and made a leader from white tape and wrote a label in her own hand, not his.

She played it through once at quarter speed for the catalog notes, the way she would have done in October if she had been honest. At quarter speed the storm became a room, and the voices became structures, and the hiss became what it had always been: the tape itself, the oxide and the binder and the long fall of a signal into air. She listened for an hour with the headphones on and heard nothing new. She listened again the next day, and there was still nothing in it but weather and men and the sea.

On the Sunday before Christmas she drove Emeka out to Salt Row. He sat in the passenger seat with his hands on his knees and did not talk, and the tape deck was still broken, and the heater clicked and roared. The house looked small under the wet sky, the paint grey with salt, the porch light on although it was afternoon.

Adaeze opened the door before they reached the step. She stood in the doorway with her cardigan held closed at the throat and looked at her son for a long moment, and the rain ticked in the gutter, and gulls argued over something on the seawall. "You're late," she said. Then she stepped back and let him in. Nadia stayed on the step. Through the glass she watched the two of them sit down at the kitchen table, and the kettle go on, and her mother's hand come out across the oilcloth and stop there, not quite touching his sleeve.

Rusk Marine Holdings took its name off the trailer at Kestrel Point in January. The station was fenced and listed for survey, the mast standing, the transmitter room sealed with a padlock and a notice that said nothing about the log inside it.

Serrano called her twice that winter, each time with less in his voice than the time before, and the second time he said the county had charged Desmond Rusk with three counts and that Arthur Rusk had died at Bellwether Hill on a Tuesday without ever being asked a question in a room with a recorder in it.

Fiona came by the archive with coffee in paper cups and a folder of the Anselm's salvage records, which she read the way other people read novels. She had started writing something about the wreck, and she read Nadia a paragraph of it, and the paragraph was good, and Nadia said so. Gus retired in February, or said he would, and then came in three days a week anyway, and ate his licorice drops, and did not once ask her about the line in the register.

The fog came in off the water on the last Friday of the month and filled Fenwick Street. Nadia finished the last of the catalog notes and closed the ledger and slid it back onto its shelf, and stood with her hand flat on the red spine. Somewhere out past the Narrows a ferry sounded, one long note, and the sound came down the basement stairs and into the room and settled in the corners with the dust.

She set the tape box on the shelf in its place, between the harbor commission minutes and the weather bureau ledgers, where anyone could find it. The archive was quiet. The radiator ticked. From the speakers of the Revox, with the volume low and the reel threading through for one last check, came the faint, even hiss of the tape, and it was just hiss now, nothing under it, nothing waiting to be found.
