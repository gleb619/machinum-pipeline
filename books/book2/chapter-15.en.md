# Chapter 15: The Green Float

The float was on the porch when she got home, sitting on the top step beside the mat with the rain beading on it. Green glass, the size of a grapefruit, wrapped in a cradle of knotted netting that had gone stiff with salt. She stopped with her key in the door and looked at it for a long time before she picked it up.

There was a note folded under the netting, damp at one corner, in pencil on a page torn from a pocket notebook. It said: "I am here. Come quietly." And beneath that, in the same careful hand: "Cinder Yards. Friday. 11:40."

She stood on her own porch in the rain with the note in her hand and the float heavy and cold against her palm, and something in her chest turned over and lay still.

She and Emeka had found the first one on the beach below Kestrel Point when she was eight and he was twelve, a Japanese float that had crossed the whole Pacific and washed up at their feet among the crab shells and the driftwood. They had kept it on the windowsill of the room they shared, where the light came through it green and moving on the wall.

And afterward, when they were older and he was working nights at the station and she was a teenager who could not sleep until she heard his key in the door, they had made it a signal. A green float meant I am here, come quietly. It meant do not call, do not tell anyone, do not make a sound. It meant the thing was happening and she should not be afraid.

Nobody else knew that. Not her mother. Not Fiona. Not Gus. It was a language with two speakers, and one of them had been dead for twelve years, and now the float sat in her hand on a wet autumn evening and the language was alive again.

She carried it inside and set it on the kitchen table under the light, and turned it, and watched the green deepen and thin where the glass was thick. Salt was crusted in the knots of the netting. There was a smear of engine grease on one side, as though it had been carried in a toolbox.

She thought of the bench in Cape Verity, and of a man setting down an iron before he ran, and of the bell over the door still ringing. She thought of Desmond Rusk in the corridor at Bellwether Hill, discussing the weather. She thought of the house on Salt Row, and the loan, and the way Adaeze had refused her son's name for twelve years, and how a Friday night in the Cinder Yards could take all of that away or give it back.

She made tea and did not drink it. She sat with the note flattened under a book and read it until the pencil seemed to say other things.

Friday was two days away. She had a shift at the archive on Thursday and she would go to it, and she would catalog what was on her desk, and she would say nothing to anyone, not to Fiona, not to Serrano, not to her mother.

Because that was the part of the signal that had always mattered most: come quietly. Whoever Emeka was now, whatever the twelve years had cost, he had chosen a language only the two of them could read, and he had chosen the Cinder Yards, where they had caught tadpoles in the spring pools between the rusted rails, where nobody went at night except kids and foxes and the wind.

She got up and took her coat off the hook and hung it properly, and set her wet shoes by the door, and turned off the light in the front room. The float stayed on the table, green and quiet, glowing faintly in the dark like a small lamp, like a signal that had crossed a very long water to reach her.

She would go. Of course she would go.
