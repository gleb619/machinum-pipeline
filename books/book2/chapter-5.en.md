# Chapter 5: Kestrel Point

She went on a Saturday, early, before the archive opened. The coast road north out of Marrow Bay climbed through wet spruce and ran along the top of the cliffs, and the Volvo's wipers beat a slow argument with the rain. Salt fogged the windshield faster than the defroster could clear it. She drove with both hands and hummed, very quietly, a tune she did not know she knew.

Kestrel Point was a knuckle of black rock with a stand of wind-bent pines behind it, and the station sat on the crown of it like something left behind by a tide. One mast, guy-wired, leaning. A low cinderblock building with a flat roof, a chain-link fence gone orange with rust. The sign by the gate still said KSTL 1140 in letters faded to the color of old newspaper.

The padlock had been cut. Not long ago; the shackle was bright where the bolt cutters had sheared it, and the hasp still held a curl of metal like a fishhook. She stood looking at it with the rain running off her hood and felt the humming move up into her throat.

The tire tracks were fresh. Two ruts of chewed gravel ran through the gate and around the side of the building, set wide apart, the tread of something heavier than a pickup. Rain had softened their edges but not erased them. A week old, maybe less.

She parked the Volvo off the road behind the pines, where it could not be seen from the gate, and went in on foot. The door was not locked. Inside, the air was cold and mineral, wet concrete and mouse and the ghost of old cigarette smoke. Water ticked somewhere in the dark. Her flashlight found a corridor, a bulletin board with a curled memo, a coffee cup on a shelf with a skin of something grown in it.

The transmitter room was at the back. The big RCA had been hauled out years ago and left a pale rectangle on the floor and a shadow on the wall. In its place, on a steel bench, sat a low-power emergency transmitter, forty watts at most, its case scratched and its meters dark. A cable ran from it up through a hole in the ceiling toward the mast. There was no dust on the cable.

She crouched and swept the light along the wall, and the beam caught the edge of a panel that did not sit flush with the others. She got her fingers behind it. It came away with a sound of old screws giving up, and behind it, in the cavity between the studs, lay a logbook.

It was a duplicate working log, cloth-bound, swollen with damp, the pages ruled in blue. She opened it on the bench and turned to March. The entries were in several hands and one of them she knew. Every hour of the night of the third was there, carrier on, power steady, storm static heavy, mast holding, and in the margin beside the last entry, in pencil, a single word: kept.

She photographed every page with her phone, steadying it against the bench, and then she put the book inside her coat, against her ribs.

The truck came up the gravel while she was still in the corridor. She heard it before she saw it, a heavy engine in low gear, tires crunching, the particular idling cough of a diesel. Through the window at the end of the hall she saw white, and along the white door the words RUSK MARINE HOLDINGS in blue.

She killed the flashlight and went into the little room with the dead coffee cup, and pressed herself into the corner behind the door, and hummed without sound, her lips against her own wrist.

Two men got out. Their boots were loud on the concrete. They walked the perimeter first, slow, unhurried, and one of them tried the gate hasp and laughed at something. Their voices came through the wall without words, only the shape of them. Then the footsteps came inside and stopped at the transmitter room, and stayed a while, and went out again.

She counted to four hundred after the engine faded, then counted to two hundred more, then made herself walk out into the rain. The truck's tracks overlaid her own at the gate. She drove the Volvo out the long way, around the back of the pines, and did not turn on the headlights until she was a mile down the coast road.

The logbook rode under the passenger seat, wrapped in a plastic grocery bag, and every few miles she reached across and touched it through the upholstery to be sure it was still there. Rain came at the windshield sideways. A ferry horn sounded somewhere out in the fog, two long notes, and she hummed back at it, and drove south toward home.
