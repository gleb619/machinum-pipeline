# Chapter 19: The Storm

The storm came in from the southwest all afternoon, and by nine the rain was going sideways against the archive windows and the radio was reading out closures in a flat, unhurried voice. Nadia stood in the basement doorway with her coat already on and listened to the coast road being taken away, mile by mile, the way the tide takes a beach.

They met in the lot behind the library. Fiona drove, because Fiona did not argue with weather. Gus came out of the side door with a canvas tool bag and a thermos and his boots laced tight, sixty-one years old and moving like a man who had somewhere to be. Serrano arrived last, in a raincoat too new for the season.

"I want it understood," he said, "that I am not here as a police officer. I have no warrant and no authority past the city line." "Understood," Nadia said. "I am not going to stop you," he said, and got in the truck.

The coast road ran north with the sea on the left, close enough that spray came over the guardrail and the wipers could not keep up. Fiona drove at forty and said nothing. In the back, Gus held the tool bag on his knees and watched the dark as though he could read it.

Four miles short of the point, a sheriff's truck stood across the lane with its blue lights turning, and beyond it a stand of alder lay down on the asphalt with its roots in the air. The deputy leaned to the window and asked, not unkindly, what they thought they were doing. Serrano showed him something and said something, and the deputy looked at the four of them for a long moment, then moved the truck a yard and let them by. Nadia did not ask what he had shown.

The service track was mud and gravel and old cinders. The truck went up it sideways, and twice Fiona corrected with the flat of her hand and no drama at all. Headlights swung across water, then grass, then the low black shape of the station on its point, the mast standing out of it into the rain.

In the beam, the demolition yard was already there. An excavator on a flatbed. A light tower, dark. A white trailer with a generator humming beside it, and a sign that said RUSK MARINE HOLDINGS in letters that did not care about weather. The crew would come at first light, and by eight the mast would be down and the room behind it rubble.

They parked a quarter mile short and walked the rest, hoods up, flashlights low. Gus led, because he had walked that ground for nine years and his feet remembered it even if his knees did not.

The door was not locked, which was its own kind of insult. Inside, the air smelled of mildew and old cigarettes and, beneath that, ozone, the ghost of a thousand small electricals. Their beams found a counter, a chair, a wall of grey steel cabinets.

"The log will be in the desk," Gus said, in the voice of a man saying a thing he dreaded. Nadia pulled the bottom drawer and it came out crooked, warped with damp, and there was the bound volume, water-marked and heavy, its rules drawn in a careful engineer's hand. Gus had given her the pages he had torn out and kept. Here was the book they had come from.

She put it in the canvas bag, then touched her coat pocket, where the master reel sat wrapped in a sock and the small recorder held Vera's voice. The reel taken from her office had been Doyle's copy. This one had not left her brother's hands in twelve years.

Then the work lamp the crew had strung in the corridor died. Out in the yard the trailer's generator stopped, and its hum had been there so long she had stopped hearing it, and the dark came in like water. Gus made a sound that was almost relief. "Standby set," he said. "There's a diesel in the lean-to. Bring the torch."

He was out in the rain a long time. The wind worked at the roof. Once, headlights swept the window and a truck went along the point road, slowing at the trailer and going on, and the four of them crouched below the sill until the red taillights were gone. Serrano's hand stayed on the radio at his belt.

Then the lean-to door banged and the diesel coughed and caught, and the room came up yellowish, and Gus stood in the doorway dripping, holding a rag and a screwdriver, with the look of a man who had won a small argument with machinery.

The emergency transmitter was a low-power board the size of a suitcase, bolted to a shelf of its own under a dust sheet. Above it, screwed to the wall, a plate: KSTL-AM 1140. Nadia ran cable from the Revox to the board, then from the recorder, and checked the levels twice; the needles moved and settled.

Serrano watched the road. Fiona held the flashlight. Nadia put her hand on the switch and let out the breath she had been carrying since the library door. The relay closed. The needles lifted, trembled, held, and the transmitter began to hum.
