# Chapter 14: Cape Verity

The invoice was at the bottom of the fourth box, folded twice and soft at the creases from years of being carried in a pocket. Ward Marine Electronics, Cape Verity. A VHF repair, an antenna mount, a depth sounder that had needed a new transducer. Cormac Doyle's name in the customer line, dated the spring of 2017 — three years after the Pelargic was found empty and her brother was declared drowned.

On the back, in pencil, in handwriting she had last seen on a reel label in a biscuit tin: "Ask for Eli. He knows the boat."

Nadia sat on the archive floor for a long time with the invoice in her hands and the fluorescent light buzzing overhead, and then she stood up, and put on her coat, and told Gus she was taking a personal day.

She left before dawn. The coast road ran north along the water, and the fog lay on it in gray sheets, and a ferry horn sounded somewhere out past the point, low and long, the way it did on the mornings she used to wait for Emeka to come home from the station. Ninety miles. She stopped once for coffee and did not drink it. The tape deck in the Volvo was broken, so she drove in silence, and the silence had a hiss to it.

Cape Verity was smaller than Marrow Bay and seemed to lean away from the sea. The boats in the basin were working boats, crabbers and a couple of tired trawlers, and the smell of diesel and kelp came off the docks and followed her up the main street. Gulls stood on the pilings like men in gray coats, waiting.

Ward Marine Electronics was a narrow storefront between a laundromat and a shuttered chandlery, with a hand-painted sign and a window full of radio parts arranged with care. Through the glass she could see the bench, the yellow light of a work lamp, the spine of a soldering iron held at an angle, and a man bent over a circuit board with a magnifier clipped to his glasses.

She knew him before he turned. She knew him by the set of his shoulders and the way he held the iron, delicate as a pen, and by the small habit of tilting his head to hear the set he was working on, as though the radio were speaking to him and not the other way around.

Then he turned.

He was thirty-eight now. There was gray in his beard and a stiffness in his left knee when he shifted his weight, and a scar along his jaw that had not been there before. But his face did the thing she remembered, the brief widening before he decided what to feel, and for one full second he was simply her brother, looking at her across twelve years.

Then he was not. He looked at the door, and at her, and at the glass, and she saw him understand everything at once and choose.

He set the soldering iron down very carefully, as though it mattered, and then he went through the back door so fast the bell over it had not finished ringing before he was gone. She heard boots on wet gravel, then a gate, then nothing but the hiss of the radio on the bench and the smell of hot solder and dust.

Nadia stood in the empty shop with her heart going like a hammer. She did not run after him. She knew, with a certainty that surprised her, that running would only send him further.

She found a pencil on the bench and turned the invoice over and wrote her number on the back, and beneath it, "Em. It's Nadia. I'm not here to make you come home." Then she folded it once and slid it under the back door, into the alley, where the rain was coming down again.

She drove south with the heater going and the wipers slapping. The fog had lifted off the water and the last light lay in long copper bars across the bay. She thought about the way he had set down the iron before he ran, carefully, the way he did everything, and about the gray in his beard, and about the fact that he had not said a word to her, and that the silence had been, in its way, the most eloquent thing he had ever done.

He was alive. He was ninety miles up a coast road and he was alive, and he had known her in the time it took to look up, and he had run because he was afraid for her and not of her. She held the wheel with both hands and let the certainty settle into her like warmth coming back into her fingers.
