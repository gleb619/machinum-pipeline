# Chapter 11: Gus's Story

They waited until the last researcher had gone and the library upstairs had dimmed to its night lights. Gus made tea on the hot plate, two chipped mugs, and set one in front of Nadia without asking, and she wrapped her hands around it.

"You want the whole of it," he said. "I do." He nodded slowly. "Then put your notebook away. This isn't a thing I can say twice."

He told it looking at the middle of the table, not at her. On the night of 3 March 2014 the storm came down the coast faster than anyone had forecast, and Kestrel Point lost its lights at eight and got them back at nine. The station was a hundred watts and a prayer, but it was on the air.

"At eleven forty the call came in," Gus said. "Not to the station. To the Coast Guard, on the emergency band, and Emeka had the scanner in the booth like always. A ship calling Mayday. The Anselm. She was supposed to have gone down two weeks before, official and tidy. And here she was, still afloat, taking water." His hands were steady, which was somehow worse.

"There was a man on that radio. Not the captain. A man with a voice like a closed door. He gave a position, and then he said, 'Kestrel Point, stay off the air. This is not happening.' Like he was ordering a taxi." "And Emeka?" "Emeka had the tape running before the man finished the first sentence." Gus almost smiled. "He didn't ask permission. He heard a voice, hit record, and sat with his hand on the machine while a ship died."

Nadia had stopped breathing. She made herself sip the tea, too hot and tasting of the pot. "You were in the room." "I was at the transmitter. I came back and found him white as paper, with the reel going round."

"Then the phone rang. Not the emergency line. The office line, the one nobody had the number for. A man said there would be a weather log entry for the night. Storm damage, transmitter down, off the air from eight until morning. He said it would be in the book by the time we came in, and it was. He said we were good men and should stay good men."

"Did you report it?" The silence went on a long time. "I had a wife," Gus said at last. "A mortgage, a pension twelve years from the line. Emeka had a sister and a mother and a temper." He looked at her then, and his eyes were wet. "We made a bargain in that room. He would keep the tape and say nothing, and I would keep the log. And then the boat came in empty and your brother was gone, and I have kept my half of a bargain with a dead man for twelve years, and it has not bought me one night's sleep."

She reached across the table and put her hand over his. Rain ticked at the high windows. Neither of them spoke.

"There's more," Gus said. He went to the shelf behind his desk, to a row of bound annual reports nobody had opened in years, and from a hollow behind one spine he took a folded sheaf of pages, their edges soft and water-stained. "Pages from the station master log. Every sign-on and sign-off in the engineer's own hand. The book itself never left the station — it should still be in the desk there, unless the damp has taken it. I tore out the week of the third before the place closed and kept them. The official copy in the city files came out saying we were off the air."

He unfolded them and turned them toward her. In a careful engineer's script, dated 3 March 2014: sign-on at 18:02, the storm, the drop at 20:14, the restore at 21:07, and then the line she had waited twelve years to read. 23:40, carrier on, continuous, to 06:00. Signed.

"It proves the station was broadcasting," she said. "It proves the log is a lie," Gus said. "Not what they broadcast, or what happened to the ship. But it puts a crack in the wall." He pushed the pages across the table. "I'm sixty-one. I've got one good thing left to do. Take it."

She put both hands on the pages like a woman taking communion. They were heavier than they looked. Somewhere in the fiber of the paper was the night her brother had chosen to keep a tape instead of a job, and then chosen to disappear instead of explain.

"Why now?" she asked. "Why not twelve years ago?" Gus thought about it. "Because twelve years ago I was afraid of the man with the closed-door voice. And then I watched you come to work every day and hang your coat on the same hook and never say his name, and I understood that being afraid is a thing you can do forever or a thing you can stop doing." He picked up the mugs. "I'm old, Nadia. I would like to stop."

They washed the mugs and stood together by the basement door, and the rain had thinned to a fine cold mist. A ferry horn sounded out on the water. She held the pages against her coat, inside her arms.
