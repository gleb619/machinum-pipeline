# Chapter 10: The Break-In

The rain had been at it since before dawn, and the basement door of the Carnegie library stood open an inch when Nadia came down Fenwick Street with her key already in her hand. She knew before she touched it. Eleven years of cataloguing had taught her the sound of a thing out of place.

She went in sideways and found the lights. The fluorescents stuttered and held, and she could see the damage from the end of the stacks: her office door open, the desk drawers pulled and dropped, the filing boxes dumped into a drift of paper. Somebody had searched with a methodical anger, the way a person does when they already know what they are looking for.

Her chair was on its side. The Revox A77 sat where it always sat, its lid closed, but the take-up reel was empty and the biscuit tin she had kept on the shelf behind it was gone. Not the papers in the tin. The tin. The quarter-inch reel labeled in her brother's handwriting, which she had never once logged in the register.

She stood very still and listened to the building. Rain in the downspouts. A bus hissing past on Fenwick. Her own blood. The reel was gone, and the laptop was not, because she had taken it home the night before without thinking, the way you take an umbrella. Now she thought about it, and her hands began to shake, and she put one flat on the desk until they stopped.

Gus found her there. He came down the aisle in his cardigan with his keys still jingling and stopped when he saw the floor, and his face went pale and then gray, like paper held too close to a flame. "Nadia," he said. "Are you hurt?" "I'm not hurt. They took the reel."

He did not ask what reel. That was the first thing she noticed. A supervisor who had spent thirty years in the archive should have asked what reel; instead he looked at the empty shelf and then at his shoes and put a licorice drop in his mouth and did not offer her one.

The police came and took photographs and a statement and were gone inside an hour. Nothing else was missing. Not the petty cash, not the computers, not the harbor authority ledgers on permanent loan. Only the reel, and the report that said it did not exist.

That afternoon the complaint arrived. Gus brought it to her desk on a single sheet of letterhead, holding it by the corner as though it were wet. Rusk Marine Holdings alleged that a municipal employee had been removing and mishandling records on permanent loan, and requested a review of the archive's staffing. The city would decide within the week whether her position, which was not permanent, would continue.

"They're going to fire me," Nadia said. "They're going to try," Gus said. "It's not the same thing." But he would not meet her eyes.

She asked him the question sideways, the way you approach a damaged tape. "Gus. In 2014. Where were you working?" He was quiet so long the fluorescent light seemed to get louder. Then he sat down in the chair across from her, heavily, all at once, like a man giving up a wall he had been holding for a long time.

"Kestrel Point," he said. "I was the engineer on duty. The night of the storm." She did not move. "You knew my brother." "I knew Emeka." His voice was dry. "He was the best night man that station ever had. He could hear a bad splice through a wall." He turned a licorice drop over in his fingers and did not eat it. "I've known who you were since the day you applied. There was never a good way to say it, and then there was never any way at all."

The rain had stopped and the basement had gone very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a librarian rolled a cart across the floor, a sound like distant thunder. Nadia looked at the wreck of her office, at the papers the police had stepped over, at the empty shelf. "They took the tape," she said slowly, "but they didn't take the logbook." The engineer's log from Kestrel Point was in the drawer of her grandmother's sewing table, under the patterns, and no one had come looking for it.

"Then they don't know everything," Gus said. "No." She picked up her chair and set it upright and sat down in it, and the light buzzed above her like a held note. "They came into an archive at night to steal a reel of tape they could have ignored. You don't break into a basement for a thing you're not afraid of."

Outside, a ferry horn sounded through the wet streets, low and long, on its way out past the point. She thought of Emeka in the dark of a radio booth, headphones clamped to his ears, hearing something he was not supposed to hear. The theft was a mistake. Whoever had done it had told her, without meaning to, that the tape still mattered, and that somewhere a man could not sleep for knowing it existed.
