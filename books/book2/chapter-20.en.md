# Chapter 20: Eleven Forty

The clock on the transmitter-room wall had stopped at 11:40 sometime in 2014 and nobody had ever wound it. Nadia looked at it while the valves warmed, and then she pulled the microphone over and said her name. "This is Nadia Okonkwo, archivist at the Marrow Bay Municipal Archive. What follows was recorded in this room on the third of March, 2014."

She cued the master reel and let it run. The hiss came first, the old sea-sound of quarter-inch tape, and under the hiss the storm that had stood over the coast for three days. Then the carrier. Then a voice from a ship that had already sunk in the records, calling for help it would not get. Then a man's voice, flat and unhurried, telling someone to put it down. Then her brother, whispering her name, telling her not to look for him but to look at the water.

Between the segments she read from the bound log, holding the page flat with two fingers. The dates. The hours. The signature of an engineer named G. Pemberton. Every line of it said the station was on the air that night, for nearly twelve hours, when the official record said otherwise.

Then she played the small recorder, holding it close so the cable would not pull, and Vera Lindqvist spoke into the room out of a hospice afternoon. Her voice was dry and careful and unhurried, the voice of a woman setting a thing down at last. The scuttling of the Anselm. The payment to the Renn family. Arthur Rusk's order, given once and never written. The order to bury the Pelargic inquiry, and the green glass float on a windowsill. Behind her, faintly, a trolley went past in a corridor.

What the city heard, Nadia could not know. But the console's old telephone began to ring, on a line that should have been dead since the station closed, and Serrano's radio filled with dispatch asking, twice, who was broadcasting on 1140. Fiona's phone lit and lit again on the counter. Out past the black window, something was happening in the dark beyond the point, though whether it was the storm or the town she could not tell.

She kept talking. She read the name Tobias Renn and the settlement paid quietly to his mother. She read the dates of the inquiry that had opened and closed inside a week. She said the word fraud plainly into the microphone, in her own voice.

Headlights turned off the point road and came up the track and stopped beside the excavator. A car door. Then Desmond Rusk was walking the last of the mud with no umbrella, his coat dark to the shoulder, his shoes ruined, and she understood that he had come out early to see the site before the crew and had heard the radio instead.

He stood in the doorway with the rain running off him and looked at the microphone and then at her. "Turn it off," he said. His voice was quiet. He was not the man from the corridor at Bellwether Hill; he had lost the umbrella and the talk about weather. "You don't understand what you're doing. My father is a sick old man. There are families in this town who have lived with this for twelve years. You think you're helping them?"

She did not answer him. She held the microphone out toward him, and his voice went out over the county, and he heard himself in the monitor speaker and stopped.

"Our family lived with it too," Nadia said, and turned back to the board.

Serrano came across the room with his coat open and his badge already out, and his voice was flat and tired and certain. He told Rusk he was under arrest, and he named the things plainly: tampering with evidence, obstruction, conspiracy. He said the words a police officer says, and Rusk did not resist him, and did not look at any of them again.

Then Serrano keyed the radio, asked for a car and the county prosecutor's office, and requested a hold on the demolition permit until further notice. He said the 2014 file was reopened at his request and would be reopened formally at eight. When he said it his voice changed, just slightly, like a man setting down something heavy.

The reel ran out; the tape lifted and began to slap against the take-up spool. Nadia stopped it, then read the final page of the log into the microphone, and then she said that both recordings would be held at the archive, catalogued, and available to anyone who asked. She said her brother's name, in full, without whispering it.

Outside the window the dark had gone grey at the edges. The demolition crew's trucks came up the point road at first light and stopped where the police car stood across the track, and the foreman sat with his engine idling a while, then shut it off. The excavator did not start.

Nadia pulled the switch. The needles dropped, the carrier died, and the hiss went out of the room. She stood at the window with her hands on the cold sill and watched the first light come up on the water, grey and then pearl, and the sea making its long, indifferent noise against the rocks.
